Secondary Hypertension

A practical, integrated reference for the diagnosis and treatment of primary aldosteronism and other secondary causes of hypertension — combining AHA/ACC 2025, Endocrine Society 2025, BIHS 2023/2024, and JAMA IM 2026 guidance.

⚠️ Educational reference only. This tool summarizes published guidelines for clinician reference. It is not a substitute for clinical judgment, institutional protocol, or the full guideline documents. Assay-specific cutoffs must be verified against your local laboratory.

Why it matters

Secondary causes are present in ~5–25% of adults with hypertension. Primary aldosteronism is the most common identifiable cause — and it is potentially curable (unilateral disease) or specifically targetable (bilateral disease with MRAs).

The four key sources

AHA/ACC 2025 — targeted case detection with Class 1 indications.
Endocrine Society 2025 — universal ARR screening position.
BIHS 2023/2024 — UK practical approach addressing local variation.
JAMA IM 2026 — critical review questioning ARR and suppression testing reliance.

The core triad (PA)

Diagnosis rests on demonstrating suppressed renin + inappropriately elevated aldosterone + an elevated ARR — then confirming, then lateralizing.

🌍 International note

Aldosterone and renin are reported in different units around the world — ng/dL vs pmol/L, PRA vs DRC, per hour vs per second. The ARR cutoff only makes sense in the units it was derived in. Use the Unit Converter section to translate any result into any system.

The Full PA Pathway at a Glance

1
Case detection — apply screening criteria (Class 1 indications per AHA/ACC; universal per Endocrine Society; pragmatic per BIHS).
▼
2
Biochemical screening — morning, seated, liberal sodium, potassium normalized. Measure PAC + PRA (or DRC) → calculate ARR.
▼
3
Confirmatory testing — aldosterone suppression: IV saline suppression test (SIT), oral salt loading, captopril challenge, or fludrocortisone suppression. Role now debated.
▼
4
Subtyping — adrenal CT plus adrenal venous sampling (AVS) to distinguish unilateral from bilateral disease.
▼
5
Treatment — unilateral → laparoscopic adrenalectomy; bilateral or nonsurgical → mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist.
🇬🇧 BIHS perspective: There is a continuum between low-renin essential hypertension and PA. The classic Conn syndrome (hypertension + hypokalemia + alkalosis) is found in only ~1/3 of cases — adenoma with normokalemia is the most common presentation. PA is also an independent cardiovascular risk factor (stroke, CAD, AF, heart failure, diabetes, metabolic syndrome).

Who to Screen

AHA/ACC 2025 Class 1 and Class 2b indications, the Endocrine Society's broader position, and the BIHS pragmatic approach.

AHA/ACC 2025 — Screen These Adults Class 1

Screen adults with hypertension if any of the following are present:

  • Resistant hypertension — regardless of whether hypokalemia is present
  • Hypokalemia — spontaneous or diuretic-induced
  • Obstructive sleep apnea
  • Incidentally discovered adrenal mass
  • Family history of early-onset hypertension
  • Stroke at a young age (<40 years)

May also consider Class 2b in adults with stage 2 hypertension.

General Suspicion Triggers for Any Secondary Cause

  • Stage 2 hypertension
  • Treatment-resistant hypertension
  • Sudden-onset hypertension
  • Loss of previously good control
  • Early-onset hypertension

A careful medication/substance history is essential — prescription, OTC, herbal, and illicit agents can all raise BP.

Endocrine Society 2025 — The Broader Position Conditional

The updated Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline goes further than AHA/ACC, suggesting screening all individuals with hypertension — a move toward universal case detection. This reflects evidence that PA is far more prevalent than previously recognized and that hypokalemia is absent in the majority of cases.

BIHS 2023/2024 — The UK Pragmatic View Consensus

The BIHS document acknowledges that UK National guidelines do not provide extensive PA recommendations, and local practice varies. Its aim is to raise awareness, guide screening/diagnosis, and support treatment to mitigate adverse cardiometabolic effects in otherwise undiagnosed individuals. The BIHS emphasizes that PA remains often unrecognized, particularly in resistant hypertension.

Why the Guidelines Disagree

💡 Practical reconciliation: In settings where ARR testing is readily available, the Endocrine Society's broader approach is defensible. In resource-constrained settings, the AHA/ACC Class 1 list captures the highest-yield population — resistant hypertension, hypokalemia, adrenal incidentaloma, and young stroke remain the strongest triggers.

Biochemical Screening

How to screen — the ARR, assay-specific thresholds, pre-test optimization, and medication management.

The Core Principle Class 1

Use plasma aldosterone concentration (PAC), plasma renin activity (PRA), and the aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR) together. This combined interpretation is currently the most accurate and reliable screening method. The ARR alone should not be used — it compounds the measurement error of both assays.

Medication Management

✅ Continue most antihypertensives

The AHA/ACC guideline advises continuing most antihypertensives during initial screening to minimize barriers to testing — including ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and CCBs. Do not let medication logistics prevent screening.

⚠️ Beta-blockers and central alpha-agonists

These suppress renin and may cause false positives, but results remain interpretable in most cases. Hold for 2 weeks if clinically safe and if the result would change management.

✘ The exception: MRAs

Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists should ideally be withdrawn for at least 4 weeks before testing. They directly block the receptor, raise renin, and lower aldosterone — reliably producing a false negative.

🔄 If results are negative or borderline with high suspicion

Substitute interfering agents with non-interfering agents for 2–4 weeks and repeat testing:

Sampling Tips & Pre-Test Checklist

A practical, step-by-step guide to getting the aldosterone and renin sample right the first time — where most diagnostic errors originate.

💡 Why this matters: The ARR is exquisitely sensitive to pre-analytical conditions. A sample drawn in the wrong posture, at the wrong time, with the wrong potassium, or on the wrong medications can produce a completely misleading result — often a false negative that delays diagnosis for years.

1. Timing & Posture — Get This Right First

⏰ Timing
  • Morning draw — ideally between 8:00 and 10:00 AM
  • Aldosterone and renin both follow a circadian rhythm, peaking in the early morning
  • Avoid late-afternoon draws — aldosterone may be physiologically lower
  • Ensure the patient has been up and about (not recumbent overnight) before the draw
🪑 Posture
  • Seated for 5–15 minutes before venipuncture
  • Same posture for both aldosterone and renin sampling
  • Avoid prolonged recumbency — it suppresses renin and raises the apparent ARR
  • Ambulation before the draw is acceptable and preferred over lying down

2. Potassium — The Most Common False-Negative Cause

🧂 Potassium rules
  • Correct hypokalemia before testing. Hypokalemia falsely lowers aldosterone — a hypokalemic patient with PA can look biochemically normal.
  • Measure potassium alongside aldosterone — the two must be interpreted together.
  • If the screen is negative in a hypokalemic patient, correct potassium and repeat the test before excluding PA.
  • Potassium supplements and potassium-sparing diuretics (amiloride, triamterene) can be used to normalize K⁺ while awaiting testing — they do not invalidate the ARR.

3. Sodium Intake — Do Not Restrict

🧂 Sodium rules
  • Unrestricted, liberal salt intake for at least 3 days before testing.
  • Sodium restriction raises renin and aldosterone — obscuring the suppressed-renin pattern of PA.
  • No need to formally sodium-load for the initial screen — just don't restrict.
  • A 24-hour urinary sodium can confirm adequate intake if the result is ambiguous.

4. Drug Holds — The Practical Cheat Sheet

🛑 HOLD before testing (when clinically safe)
  • Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists — the most important hold.
    • Spironolactone: 4–6 weeks (long half-life, active metabolites)
    • Eplerenone: 2–4 weeks
  • Beta-blockers — suppress renin → false positives. Hold 2 weeks if BP allows.
  • Central alpha-agonists — clonidine, methyldopa. Suppress renin. Hold 2 weeks if safe; clonidine rebound is a risk — taper.
  • NSAIDs — can raise potassium and blunt renin. Hold 1–2 weeks if possible.
  • Licorice, carbenoxolone, chewing tobacco — mineralocorticoid-like effect. Stop 2 weeks before.
  • Potassium-wasting diuretics — hold 2 weeks if BP allows, but they can be continued if needed for BP control; interpret renin cautiously.
✅ CONTINUE during testing (per AHA/ACC 2025)
  • ACE inhibitors / ARBs — continue. They may raise renin, but the AHA/ACC advises continuing to minimize barriers. A suppressed renin on an ACEi/ARB is strongly suggestive of PA.
  • Calcium channel blockers — continue. Non-dihydropyridine (verapamil, diltiazem) are preferred if a substitution is needed.
  • Thiazide / loop diuretics — may be continued if needed for BP, but interpret with caution.
  • Alpha-blockers (doxazosin, prazosin, terazosin) — safe, non-interfering, useful substitutes.
  • Hydralazine — safe, non-interfering.
🔄 If a hold is not safe — substitute instead
  • Replace interfering agents with non-interfering agents for 2–4 weeks, then repeat the ARR.
  • Preferred substitutions: verapamil or diltiazem (non-DHP CCBs), doxazosin (alpha-blocker), hydralazine (vasodilator).
  • Avoid substituting with agents that themselves interfere (beta-blockers, central alpha-agonists, MRAs).

5. Sample Collection & Handling

🧪 Tube & handling
  • Collect in EDTA or heparin tube — follow your local lab's validated protocol.
  • Renin sample on ice — place the tube in an ice slurry immediately after draw.
  • Centrifuge cold (4°C) and separate plasma promptly.
  • Freeze plasma at −20°C or below if not assayed immediately.
  • Aldosterone is more stable than renin — but follow local pre-analytical requirements for both.
🧊 Common handling mistakes
  • Renin sample left at room temperature → falsely low PRA
  • Delayed centrifugation → cryoactivation of prorenin → falsely high PRA
  • Hemolyzed sample → unreliable potassium and possibly aldosterone
  • Wrong tube type (e.g., serum separator for renin) → invalid result

6. The Pre-Test Checklist — Print This

  • Patient off MRAs for ≥4 weeks (spironolactone) or ≥2 weeks (eplerenone), unless unsafe
  • Potassium normal — corrected if hypokalemic, and measured with the sample
  • Sodium intake liberal — not restricted for at least 3 days
  • Beta-blockers / central alpha-agonists held for 2 weeks if clinically safe
  • NSAIDs, licorice, carbenoxolone stopped for 1–2 weeks
  • Morning draw — 8:00–10:00 AM, patient ambulatory beforehand
  • Seated 5–15 minutes before venipuncture, same posture for both assays
  • Renin sample on ice, centrifuged cold, plasma separated promptly
  • Both aldosterone and renin sent to the lab with the correct tube types
  • Local lab cutoffs confirmed — assay type (immunoassay vs LC-MS/MS) and units known
  • If result borderline or negative with high suspicion — plan a repeat after drug substitution or potassium correction

7. Common Pitfalls — Why Screens Fail

⚠️ Top false-negative causes
  • Hypokalemia at sampling → falsely low aldosterone
  • MRAs not held → receptor blocked, renin raised, aldosterone lowered
  • Sodium restriction → renin and aldosterone both raised, ARR misleading
  • Afternoon or recumbent draw → non-diagnostic aldosterone and renin
  • Wrong assay interpretation → using an immunoassay cutoff on an LC-MS/MS result
  • Renin sample mishandled → falsely low PRA → falsely high ARR
⚠️ Top false-positive causes
  • Beta-blockers or central alpha-agonists → renin suppressed in a patient without PA
  • Advanced age or CKD → low renin is common, lowering ARR specificity
  • High aldosterone from another cause (e.g., heart failure, cirrhosis) → elevated ARR without PA
  • Renin cryoactivation → falsely high PRA (uncommon with proper handling)
💡 The bottom line: A correctly collected sample — morning, seated, liberal sodium, normal potassium, MRAs held — is worth more than any sophisticated assay. When in doubt, correct the pre-analytical conditions and repeat before excluding PA.

🌍 Unit Converter — SI ↔ US & Time Units

Translate aldosterone and renin values between international reporting systems. Because the ARR is a ratio, its value changes with units — this tool lets you see the same physiology in every system.

🚨 The #1 international interpretation error: Applying a US ARR cutoff (e.g., >30) to a result reported in SI units (pmol/L : mU/L). The same patient can have an ARR of 30 in US units and 70+ in SI units — or vice versa — depending on the unit combination. Always convert first, then apply the cutoff that matches the unit system.

⚠️ The ARR is unit-dependent — but the physiology is not

The aldosterone-to-renin ratio is a ratio of two measured concentrations. When the units of either (or both) change, the numeric ARR changes — even though the patient's actual aldosterone and renin biology has not.

🛠️ How to Use the Converter — Step by Step

  1. Enter your aldosterone value and its unit (from your lab report).
  2. Enter your renin value and its unit. Pay attention to whether your lab reports PRA (activity) or DRC (concentration), and whether time is in hours, minutes, or seconds.
  3. Read the converted values in every system — US, SI, Canada, Australia.
  4. Look up the ARR cutoff in the table above that matches the unit system of the converted values you want to use.
  5. Compare — the patient should be positive (or negative) in every consistent system. If the answer flips depending on units, your conversion has an error.

⚠️ Pitfalls Specific to International Practice

Common conversion mistakes
  • Confusing PRA and DRC. These are different analytes. PRA ng/mL/h and DRC mU/L are not interchangeable — the conversion is approximate and substrate-dependent.
  • Forgetting the time unit. ng/mL/h vs ng/mL/s is a 3600× difference. Always check whether your lab reports per hour, per minute, or per second.
  • Using the wrong ARR cutoff. A cutoff of 30 (US ng/dL:ng/mL/h) is not equivalent to 30 in SI units (pmol/L:mU/L) — the SI equivalent is ~70.
  • Mixing ng/L and ng/dL for aldosterone. 1 ng/dL = 10 ng/L. A 10× error.
✅ Safe practice
  • Always record units with any aldosterone, renin, or ARR value you document.
  • Use your own laboratory's validated cutoff whenever possible — it accounts for local assay calibration.
  • If your lab uses DRC, use DRC-based cutoffs (e.g., >70 pmol/L:mU/L); if it uses PRA, use PRA-based cutoffs (e.g., >20 or >30).
  • When in doubt, calculate the ARR in the unit system your cutoff was validated in — do not mix.
💡 The one-line rule: An ARR value without units is meaningless. An ARR cutoff applied to the wrong units is dangerous. Always convert to the unit system the cutoff was derived in — or use your local lab's validated cutoff, which already accounts for your reporting units.

Confirmatory Testing

Aldosterone suppression — and the growing debate about whether it is needed at all.

Step 1 — Refer Class 1

A positive screen — or continued suspicion (suppressed renin, disproportionate target-organ damage) — warrants referral to a hypertension specialist or endocrinologist.

Step 2 — Confirm with Aldosterone Suppression

Confirmation typically uses an aldosterone suppression test — either IV saline suppression or oral salt-loading. The physiologic principle: in normal physiology, volume expansion suppresses aldosterone; in PA, aldosterone secretion is autonomous and fails to suppress.

IV Saline Suppression Test (SIT)

The most widely used confirmatory test. 2 L of 0.9% saline infused IV over 4 hours in the recumbent position, with aldosterone measured at baseline and at completion.

Interpretation: Failure of aldosterone to suppress below the protocol threshold is consistent with PA. Exact post-infusion cutoffs vary by assay and protocol — most commonly aldosterone >10 ng/dL, though lower thresholds are used with LC-MS/MS.

Protocol specifics are standard endocrinology practice and not detailed in the retrieved sources — verify against your local laboratory protocol.

Oral Salt Loading Test

High-sodium diet for 3 days with sodium supplementation, followed by measurement of 24-hour urinary aldosterone. Adequate sodium loading is confirmed by 24-hour urinary sodium measurement.

Interpretation: Urinary aldosterone excretion that fails to suppress is consistent with PA.

Protocol specifics are standard practice and not detailed in the retrieved sources.

Captopril Challenge Test (CCT)

Oral captopril administered with aldosterone and renin measured before and after. In normal physiology, ACE inhibition lowers aldosterone; in PA, aldosterone remains elevated and the ARR does not normalize.

Simpler and safer than SIT in patients at risk of volume overload — but considered somewhat less definitive.

Protocol specifics are standard practice and not detailed in the retrieved sources.

Fludrocortisone Suppression Test (FST)

Used predominantly in Australia and some European centers. Fludrocortisone plus sodium loading over several days, with serial aldosterone measurement. Considered highly sensitive but labor-intensive and requires careful monitoring for hypokalemia and volume overload.

Protocol specifics are standard practice and not detailed in the retrieved sources.

The Evolving View: Is Confirmatory Testing Still Needed?

⚠️ Some experts and the JAMA Internal Medicine review argue the role of aldosterone suppression testing is uncertain. Reasons cited:
  • High false-negative rates — patients with genuine PA may suppress on testing and be incorrectly excluded
  • Emphasis should instead be placed on interpreting the individual renin and aldosterone values, rather than relying heavily on the ARR — which compounds the measurement error of both assays
  • Suppressed renin despite RAAS inhibitors or diuretics is itself strongly suggestive of PA, and may carry as much diagnostic weight as a suppression test

Where this leaves the clinician

The AHA/ACC and Endocrine Society guidelines still describe confirmatory testing as part of the standard pathway. The JAMA review does not advocate abandoning it, but argues it should not be treated as an absolute gatekeeper — particularly when the biochemical picture is unambiguous (markedly suppressed renin, high aldosterone, hypokalemia) or when the patient is not a surgical candidate anyway.

Also note: a strongly positive screen should prompt adrenal CT — regardless of whether confirmatory testing is pursued.

Subtyping: CT & Adrenal Vein Sampling

Distinguishing unilateral from bilateral disease — the decision that determines surgery versus medication.

🎯 The central rule: Cross-sectional imaging cannot determine functional activity. CT alone misclassifies bilateral hyperplasia and nonfunctioning nodules as unilateral adenomas. If the patient may be a surgical candidate, AVS is required regardless of what CT shows.

Why CT Alone Is Insufficient

  • CT identifies anatomy, not function
  • Nonfunctioning adrenal incidentalomas are common, especially with age
  • Bilateral hyperplasia can appear asymmetric on imaging
  • CT–AVS discordance is frequent — the JAMA review notes AVS should generally be pursued regardless of CT findings

When AVS May Be Bypassed — The Recognized Exception

Endocrine Society recognized exception: AVS may be bypassed in a patient <35 years with:
  • Marked PA (unambiguous biochemistry)
  • Hypokalemia
  • A >1 cm unilateral adenoma on CT
In this narrow scenario, the combination of young age, florid biochemistry, and a clear unilateral adenoma makes unilateral disease sufficiently likely to proceed directly to surgery.

Other situations where AVS may be skipped JAMA review

  • Patient is not interested in or not a candidate for surgery
  • Only a mildly positive ARR

In these cases, the result would not change management — the patient will receive medical therapy either way. Note that adrenal imaging should still be obtained.

Adrenal Imaging — Always Obtain CT

CT should be obtained even if medical treatment is planned — to exclude a large mass (>4 cm or with suspicious features) that could represent adrenocortical carcinoma. This is a safety measure independent of the PA workup.

Adrenal Venous Sampling — The Protocol

AVS is the gold standard for lateralization. Adrenal vein blood is sampled bilaterally and compared with a peripheral (usually IVC) sample, with cortisol used as the normalizing hormone to correct for dilution from imperfect catheter positioning.

StepDetail
Medication prepWithhold MRAs — spironolactone generally 4–6 weeks (long half-life), eplerenone ~2–4 weeks. Hypokalemia should be corrected.
TechniqueSimultaneous bilateral catheterization, or sequential sampling. Simultaneous is preferred where expertise allows.
ACTH stimulationCosyntropin infusion is commonly used to reduce stress-related fluctuations in aldosterone and improve the reproducibility of lateralization. Protocols vary — some centers do not stimulate.
SamplesRight adrenal vein, left adrenal vein, and a peripheral (IVC) sample — each measured for aldosterone and cortisol.
ComplicationsAdrenal hemorrhage and adrenal infarction are the principal risks; rates are low in experienced hands but not negligible.

AVS procedural details and numeric index thresholds reflect standard endocrine practice and the AVS literature. They are not specified in the retrieved guideline sources — always follow your local interventional radiology and endocrinology protocol.

Interpreting AVS — The Two Indices

Selectivity Index (SI)

Purpose: Confirms the catheter actually reached the adrenal vein.

SI = adrenal vein cortisol ÷ peripheral cortisol

Typical thresholds: ≥2 unstimulated, ≥3 (some protocols ≥5) with ACTH stimulation.

Lateralization Index (LI)

Purpose: Determines whether aldosterone excess is unilateral or bilateral.

LI = (A/C)dominant ÷ (A/C)non-dominant

Typical threshold: ≥4 unstimulated suggests unilateral disease. With ACTH stimulation, thresholds of ≥2 to ≥4 are used depending on protocol.

Contralateral Suppression Index (CSI)

Purpose: Supports unilateral disease by showing the opposite gland is suppressed.

CSI = (A/C)non-dominant ÷ (A/C)peripheral

A value <1 indicates suppression of the contralateral gland.

Why Thresholds Vary

LI and SI thresholds differ substantially between protocols — particularly whether ACTH stimulation is used, which assay measures aldosterone, and the reference population used to derive the cutoff. A single universal cutoff does not exist.

PA Treatment

From lateralization result to definitive management.

Decision Point: Unilateral vs Bilateral

Unilateral Excess → Surgery

Proceed to unilateral laparoscopic adrenalectomy. This is the only potentially curative option — it addresses both the hypertension and the aldosterone excess at the source.

Bilateral Disease / Nonsurgical → MRA

Treat with a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist. Bilateral hyperplasia is not amenable to surgery, and patients who decline or are unfit for surgery receive the same medical therapy.

Adrenal Imaging — Always, Regardless of Plan

CT should be obtained even if medical treatment is planned — to exclude a large mass (>4 cm or with suspicious features) that could represent malignancy. This is a cancer-safety step, not merely part of the PA subtyping workup.

Medical Therapy — Practical Points

ConsiderationDetail
First-line MRASpironolactone is the traditional first-line agent. Eplerenone is an alternative where spironolactone is not tolerated.
Spironolactone tolerabilityAnti-androgenic effects — gynecomastia, breast tenderness, menstrual irregularity, reduced libido — are dose-dependent and a common reason for switching to eplerenone.
Dose titrationTitrate to control both blood pressure and serum potassium. PA patients are often relatively resistant and may require higher doses than typical heart-failure dosing.
Potassium monitoringPotassium rises as aldosterone is blocked. Monitor closely after initiation and with each dose change — but note that PA patients tolerate this better than essential hypertension patients on MRAs.
Renin as a guideRising renin (and PRA) can be used as a marker of adequate MR blockade — a rising renin suggests the receptor is effectively blocked.

Specific MRA dosing and monitoring details are standard endocrinology practice and are not detailed in the retrieved sources.

Post-Adrenalectomy Considerations

  • Anticipate resolution of hypokalemia — potassium supplementation can usually be stopped quickly after surgery
  • Transient hyperkalemia may occur as the suppressed contralateral gland recovers — monitor closely in the early postoperative period
  • Blood pressure response is variable — some patients are cured, others improve but remain hypertensive, and a minority see no change. Duration of hypertension before surgery and the number of preoperative antihypertensives are the usual predictors.
  • Continue to monitor — biochemical cure is more reliable than blood pressure cure
💡 The therapeutic payoff: PA is the most common identifiable secondary cause of hypertension precisely because it is actionable. Unilateral disease can be cured surgically; bilateral disease can be treated with targeted medical therapy that addresses the underlying mechanism rather than merely lowering blood pressure.

Pheochromocytoma / Paraganglioma

The classic paroxysmal cause — potentially lethal if missed, but highly treatable.

When to Suspect

  • Paroxysmal hypertension — episodic, often with the classic triad of headache, palpitations, and diaphoresis
  • Adrenal mass discovered incidentally
  • Resistant hypertension
  • Young-onset hypertension
  • Paradoxical BP response to beta-blockers (unopposed alpha stimulation)
  • Family history of pheo/paraganglioma or syndromic features (MEN2, VHL, NF1, SDHx mutations)

Screening Test

Plasma free metanephrines preferred

The most sensitive test. Draw supine, after 30 minutes rest, with the patient fasting. Avoid caffeine, acetaminophen, and interfering medications for 24 hours before.

24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines

Alternative if plasma testing is not available or if the patient cannot meet supine-draw requirements. Highly specific but less sensitive than plasma free metanephrines.

Screening tests per AHA/ACC 2025 and standard endocrine practice.

Confirmatory / Next Tests

  • CT/MRI abdomen-pelvis — to localize the tumor
  • Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT — for metastatic, recurrent, or extra-adrenal disease, and for head/neck paraganglioma
  • Genetic testing — all patients with pheo/paraganglioma should be offered testing for germline mutations (SDHB, SDHD, VHL, RET, NF1, MAX, TMEM127)

Management Overview

⚠️ Critical pre-operative preparation: All patients need alpha-blockade first (phenoxybenzamine or doxazosin), followed by beta-blockade only after alpha-blockade is established — to avoid unopposed alpha stimulation and hypertensive crisis. Volume expansion with high salt intake is also essential.
  • Surgical resection is the definitive treatment for localized disease
  • Metastatic disease — treated with systemic therapy (CVD chemotherapy, MIBG, peptide receptor radionuclide therapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors)
  • Lifelong surveillance is required — recurrence rates are not trivial

Management overview is standard endocrine/surgical practice and not detailed in the retrieved guideline sources.

Cushing Syndrome

Endogenous cortisol excess — a common and under-recognized secondary cause.

When to Suspect

  • Central obesity with facial rounding (moon face) and dorsocervical fat pad (buffalo hump)
  • Proximal muscle weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs)
  • Wide purple striae (>1 cm)
  • Easy bruising
  • Hyperglycemia / diabetes
  • Osteoporosis at a young age
  • Resistant hypertension
  • Adrenal incidentaloma

Screening Test AHA/ACC

Overnight 1-mg dexamethasone suppression test

1 mg dexamethasone at 11 PM, serum cortisol at 8 AM. A normal response is cortisol <1.8 µg/dL (50 nmol/L). Values above this require further evaluation.

Confirmatory / Next Tests

  • 24-hour urinary free cortisol — two measurements recommended
  • Midnight salivary cortisol — two measurements, ideally on separate days
  • Late-night serum cortisol — if the above are equivocal
  • ACTH measurement — distinguishes ACTH-dependent (pituitary or ectopic) from ACTH-independent (adrenal) causes
  • Pituitary MRI — if ACTH-dependent
  • Adrenal CT — if ACTH-independent

Management Overview

  • Cushing disease (pituitary adenoma) — transsphenoidal surgery is first-line
  • Adrenal adenoma — laparoscopic adrenalectomy
  • Ectopic ACTH — treat the underlying tumor
  • Medical therapy — steroidogenesis inhibitors (ketoconazole, metyrapone, osilodrostat), or mifepristone for refractory cases

Management overview is standard endocrine practice and not detailed in the retrieved guideline sources.

Renovascular Disease

Renal artery stenosis — atherosclerotic or fibromuscular dysplasia.

When to Suspect

  • Resistant hypertension
  • Flash pulmonary edema — especially recurrent
  • Acute creatinine rise (>30%) after starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB
  • Kidney size asymmetry (>1.5 cm difference)
  • Abdominal bruit
  • Young-onset hypertension (fibromuscular dysplasia, especially in women 15–50)
  • Atherosclerotic disease elsewhere (CAD, PAD, carotid disease)

Screening Test

Renal duplex ultrasound

Non-invasive, no contrast, no radiation. Operator-dependent. Detects hemodynamically significant stenosis via peak systolic velocity and renal-aortic ratio.

Screening test per AHA/ACC 2025 and standard practice.

Confirmatory / Next Tests

  • CT angiography — high spatial resolution, but requires iodinated contrast (nephrotoxic risk)
  • MR angiography — avoids iodinated contrast but gadolinium risk in advanced CKD; less sensitive for FMD
  • Catheter angiography — gold standard, allows simultaneous intervention if indicated

Management Overview

  • Medical therapy — ACE inhibitors/ARBs, statins, antiplatelet therapy, BP control. Monitor creatinine and potassium closely after starting RAAS blockade.
  • Revascularization — considered for refractory hypertension, flash pulmonary edema, or deteriorating renal function. Options: percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty (PTRA) ± stenting, or surgical revascularization.
  • Fibromuscular dysplasia — PTRA without stenting is often curative
  • Atherosclerotic stenosis — medical therapy is first-line for most; revascularization reserved for specific indications

Management overview is standard nephrology/vascular practice and not detailed in the retrieved guideline sources.

Thyroid Disease & Obstructive Sleep Apnea

Two common, easily screened contributors to hypertension.

Thyroid Disease

When to suspect

  • Hyperthyroidism — weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor, palpitations, widened pulse pressure
  • Hypothyroidism — fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, diastolic hypertension
  • Atrial fibrillation — always check TSH

Screening test AHA/ACC

TSH and free thyroxine (free T4) — first-line for both hypo- and hyperthyroidism.

Confirmatory / next test

Radioactive iodine uptake (RAIU) for hyperthyroidism — distinguishes Graves disease, toxic nodular goiter, and thyroiditis.

Management overview

  • Hyperthyroidism — antithyroid drugs (methimazole, PTU), radioactive iodine, or surgery
  • Hypothyroidism — levothyroxine replacement, titrated to TSH

Obstructive Sleep Apnea

When to suspect

  • Daytime somnolence — Epworth Sleepiness Scale
  • Loud snoring reported by partner
  • Witnessed apneas
  • Resistant hypertension
  • Non-dipping nocturnal BP
  • Atrial fibrillation
  • Note: OSA is also a Class 1 screening indication for PA itself

Screening test AHA/ACC

Screening questionnaire (STOP-BANG, Epworth) and/or home polygraphy.

Confirmatory / next test

Polysomnography — gold standard; quantifies apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) and oxygen desaturation.

Management overview

  • CPAP — first-line for moderate-to-severe OSA; improves BP modestly but significantly
  • Mandibular advancement devices — for mild-moderate OSA or CPAP intolerance
  • Weight loss — often the most effective long-term intervention
  • Surgical options — for selected anatomical causes

Thyroid and OSA screening tests per AHA/ACC 2025 and standard practice. Management overview is standard practice and not detailed in the retrieved guideline sources.

Rare Causes

Less common but important secondary causes to consider when the standard workup is unrevealing.

🫀 Aortic coarctation

Suspect: Age <30 with hypertension, arm > leg BP, absent femoral pulses, systolic murmur.

Screen: Echocardiogram.

Confirm: CT/MR angiography.

🦴 Primary hyperparathyroidism

Suspect: Hypercalcemia, recurrent kidney stones, bone pain, fractures, constipation.

Screen: Serum calcium (with albumin or ionized calcium).

Confirm: PTH level — inappropriately normal or elevated PTH with hypercalcemia confirms the diagnosis.

🧬 Acromegaly

Suspect: Acral enlargement (hands, feet, jaw), headache, visual field defects, sleep apnea, hypertension, diabetes.

Screen: GH during oral glucose load, IGF-1.

Confirm: Pituitary MRI.

🧪 Non-aldosterone mineralocorticoid excess / CAH

Suspect: Low renin and aldosterone with hypokalemia; ambiguous genitalia or virilization; family history.

Screen: Urinary cortisol metabolites, genetic testing.

Confirm: Genetic testing for specific enzyme deficiencies (11β-hydroxylase, 17α-hydroxylase, etc.).

💊 Drug-induced hypertension

Suspect: Always. Review prescription, OTC, herbal, and illicit agents.

Common culprits: NSAIDs, oral contraceptives, glucocorticoids, calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine, tacrolimus), VEGF inhibitors (bevacizumab, sunitinib), sympathomimetics (decongestants, stimulants), licorice, alcohol, erythropoietin.

🧠 Other endocrine causes

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia — 11β-hydroxylase deficiency causes hypertension and virilization.

Liddle syndrome — rare monogenic hypertension with hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis; low renin and aldosterone; responds to amiloride.

Apparent mineralocorticoid excess (AME) — cortisol acts as a mineralocorticoid due to 11β-HSD2 deficiency; low renin and aldosterone; treat with spironolactone.

💡 When to consider genetic testing: Young-onset hypertension (<40), family history of early-onset hypertension or stroke, hypokalemia with metabolic alkalosis, or syndromic features suggesting a monogenic cause.

📌 Reading the table: The four sources are not in direct opposition — they represent a spectrum. AHA/ACC is the most resource-conscious; Endocrine Society is the most inclusive; BIHS provides a pragmatic UK-focused standard; the JAMA review is the most skeptical of algorithmic reliance on the ARR and suppression testing. All agree on the fundamentals: suppressed renin is the hallmark, and CT alone cannot lateralize.

Points of Universal Agreement

  • Suppressed renin is the hallmark of PA — whether measured as PRA ≤1 ng/mL/h or DRC ≤8.2 mU/L
  • Hypokalemia is not required for the diagnosis, and correcting it before testing is essential
  • Cross-sectional imaging cannot determine functional activity — CT cannot distinguish a functioning adenoma from a nonfunctioning incidentaloma
  • AVS is required before surgery in most patients, given frequent CT–AVS discordance
  • Adrenal imaging must be obtained even if medical therapy is planned, to exclude malignancy
  • Lateralization determines treatment — unilateral → adrenalectomy; bilateral → MRA
  • PA is an independent cardiovascular risk factor — not just a BP issue
